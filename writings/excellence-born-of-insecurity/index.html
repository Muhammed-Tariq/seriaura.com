Excellence, born of insecurity

"I'm writing to ask you; did you achieve all you wanted to do?"

I’m not where I want to be. I am much better. I can make myself much better.

It is wrong to think that these three statements contradict each other. We need to see that they are all true to see that a better future is possible.

The quote above only makes sense to me as a scene: I’ve suddenly been placed into a room. Within it, I spot another version of myself, but he looks a bit odd. He looks notably chubbier; not at all reminiscent of the person I’m used to looking at in the mirror. His hair is long, flowing through the sides of his head, messy yet detangled — it seems, as I suspected, to be an attempt to hide his side profile. Another, second, version of myself is also in view. This time, I can tell he’s definitely far younger than me. It’s not like I can differentiate much of his facial features — he’s wearing a mask — but I can infer that he, in a similar vein to the first version of me I spotted (whose face has now contorted in shock looking at me), had a special interest in hiding the bottom half of his face.

Like any normal subset of the human population, we immediately break into deep conversation about the state of our daily lives. It dawns on me: the first I saw is, well, me — one year younger; the other, four years further down. I tell them about what I’ve done in the past year. The work I’d done, the losses I’d suffered, the progress I’d made, my worries, my hopes and dreams, every slight and salient emotion I feel, every goal I’ve had, failed, and achieved.

While I do this, I note that their words, formerly ridden with emotion, paralysing uncertainty, and paranoia, start to sound more enthusiastic. Though I’m bad at reading emotions, I suspect I’ve painted their faces with courage, contentment, and ease — borrowed from my own. They are unfathomably proud of me, and explicitly say so. To them, I had broken so many boundaries they had struggled with for their entire life, things that had made both of them miserable throughout their lives up to that point. In retrospect, that knowledge — that a version of me would emerge with the gall to do whatever it took to shatter the life-held belief that I’d forever struggle — would have been far and away the greatest comfort to them.

In my head, I go back to when I was twelve, and I recall just how much I would have wanted to know that everything would pan out. That wanting must have been ineffable.

A sobering admission I’ve made to myself is that a lot of my achievements have been driven by the feeling that I am not good enough. I’ve observed that this stems from some vague feeling that I’m trying to overcompensate for something that I lack, that I worry is inherent — something that makes people dislike me before they’ve even met me properly. Maybe, as I’ve thought to myself, if I do more; if I make myself appear relentlessly formidable, my accomplishments impossible to dismiss; I’d be safer from judgement, I’d become more secure, and I’d be harder to reject.

This is a pretty bad theory of how relationships work, and it’s not very rational. I’d wager that a large number of people would agree with that, but also that a considerable portion of those people also nevertheless behave and feel as though that is true. The disconnect between what we know to be true and how we feel about it runs so much of our lives, and ultimately, our emotions are only crude approximations that allow us to react to things without having to reason it through — which is why my brain can know this story is wrong, and still run it anyway.

This is just one way in which we are really bad at threat-modelling. I don’t think people, when introspecting on their social dilemmas, would say: “God, nobody likes me. This must be because I haven’t benched two plates yet!” At the same time, however, things in this exact vein are exactly what people prescribe themselves when they face these struggles. As problem-solving creatures, we face an difficulty in our lives and, in order to gain some semblance of control, quickly attach a solution to this issue: a solution that, in many cases, doesn’t directly solve that problem. When the thing you want depends on other people — something you can’t control — you reach for the thing you can control; something you can do alone.

I also suspect that there are many people who chase excellence because they want to feel seen, or for a relationship-facing reason — trying to pay for human connection with the proof that you’re worth it. If you are perceived as more impressive, as better and more accomplished, more people will give you a chance, reply to you, and take you seriously, increasing your surface area for connection. It also builds a feedback loop where your competence reinforces your own confidence, and gives you an out from your rumination with structure and routine. However, status-building doesn’t guarantee you’ll reach what you actually want — it doesn’t directly get you warmth, compatibility, or connection, and it might not fix the root of your loneliness.

I don’t deny that great achievements make things better. Having great feats to talk about, being physically fit, enduring arduous journeys in life, and constantly having cool things you’re up to will make your life more exciting, more interesting to you and to other people, and most importantly, prevents you from ruminating about the perennial struggles you’ve never quite been able to fix. I would go as far as to extend this by saying that a lot of us wouldn’t have done as much as we have if it weren’t for the fact that there were external, “unsolvable” problems present in our lives.

At the same time, however, it’s well-established that you can do novel and creative things without needing to feel so terrible about yourself. It is very possible to simultaneously be completely happy, connected with and confident in the person you are now while also wanting yourself to grow and do great, exciting, and incredible things — they just aren’t mutually exclusive. One view of achievement I’ve found interesting is that of an anxiolytic — a drug that temporarily sedates the fear of inadequacy. While it’s an extremely effective substance that alleviates and distracts from your troubles, it is also incredibly addictive, and rarely tackles the root cause. Indeed, you begin to build a tolerance to it; the excellence you’d attained last year you barely perceive this year, and you push yourself to heights far greater to get the same hit.

Indeed, in mid-2024, I deeply wanted to change myself. I underwent a fitness journey that would see me losing more than 25% of my body weight. I began going to the gym, where I gained strength in almost every respect, doubling my squat and massively increasing many of my other key lifts despite losing weight. In February 2025, my school encouraged people to sign up for a half-marathon event a month away. I signed up because I had that inexpressible urge to prove that I could do whatever I set out to do, and within a month of hard training I went from barely breaking 37 minutes on the 5K to running the half in under 2 hours. In August, I finished my weight loss journey, dropping from 102kg to 72kg.

I still, now, don’t feel satisfied. I’m currently training for a sub-20-minute 5K, a sub-1:40 half-marathon, and my first full marathon two days after I turn 18, and goal creep has taken its toll on me. The goals I have empower me, but also weigh me down — they are great, and I enjoy pursuing them, but they are ultimately proxy goals for the things I value the most: meaningful human connection, transformative positive experiences, and the knowledge that I can do great things. In the end, no matter how much I achieve, I am evermore uncertain on my ability to secure those things, and it’s something I constantly think about.

This comes in complete contrast to the views of myself that my former selves would adopt of my present self, in the hypothetical I raised at the beginning. My twelve-year-old self is euphoric at the idea that I have close friends I can relate to, that my mental health is in a decent place, that I’ve had significant and life-changing positive experiences, and that I’ve been able to connect to and relate to so many people. My sixteen-year-old self is incredibly proud of me for breaking the prison I thought I was in for so long — that I would be physically unfit for my whole life. He’s amazed at how much I’ve learned how to be okay with myself, and is just as excited as I am for what I’m preparing for. Most importantly, both versions of myself feel immensely reassured that things will have gone well for me, and they’ll carry that as they continue their normal lives.

Unfortunately, I lack an older self to reassure my ruminations about the future with. It could very well have been that in the past year I’d have failed to do anything meaningful, and that therefore the worries my past selves had would’ve been justified. This is what returns me back to the proxy goals I’ve set for myself. While the numbers I chase don’t reassure me that I belong, that my future will be amazing and better than things are now, they at least tell me that I’m moving forwards, that I’m improving in some way, and that I’m not wasting my life. It’s a substitute form of certainty, and it’s not like I have a better choice.

This leaves me with a strange truth: achievement has changed my life, and it still hasn’t solved the central fear that drove it. It has made me better, and it hasn’t made me certain. It has made me impressive, and it hasn’t made me safe.

The quote at the beginning is certainly true for me. I always want more. I’m better than I was. I can always get even better. I’m still not certain about my future; I may never will be.

But in that room, the older version of me exists at all — and that is its own answer.
